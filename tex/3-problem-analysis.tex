\section{Problem analysis and requirements}
\label{sec:analysis}

Chapter~\ref{sec:sota} showed that hybrid post-quantum key exchange in IKEv2
is standardised and implemented, and that its cost has so far been measured
only for the initial establishment of an SA. This chapter turns that gap into
the problem addressed by the thesis. It states the research questions and the
scope, derives the requirements for a test bed that can answer them, selects
the IKEv2 implementation and the test platform, and fixes the configurations to
be compared. It closes with the effects that the theory of
Chapter~\ref{sec:sota} predicts, which the measurements in
Chapter~\ref{sec:results} confirm or refute.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Problem statement and research questions}
\label{sec:analysis-problem}

An operator who wants to protect IPsec traffic against a harvest-now,
decrypt-later adversary has to replace or extend the classical key exchange
of IKEv2 with ML-KEM. The standards offer two ways to do so. In a \emph{pure}
configuration, ML-KEM replaces the classical method in \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT}.
In a \emph{hybrid} configuration, a classical method stays in
\texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT} and ML-KEM follows as an additional key exchange in
\texttt{IKE\_INTERMEDIATE} (Section~\ref{sec:sota-multike}). Both are
available in released open-source daemons. What the operator does not know is
what each option costs over the lifetime of a tunnel, and under which
conditions it stops working.

The answer does not follow from the algorithm alone. ML-KEM sends one to two
orders of magnitude more key-exchange data than X25519
(Table~\ref{tab:ke-sizes}), and every additional key exchange costs one more
round trip in every procedure that performs a key exchange
(Table~\ref{tab:rtt}). Whether these bytes and round trips matter depends on
several factors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item whether the message that carries them can be fragmented at the IKE
    layer;
    \item the round-trip time, rate, loss and MTU of the link;
    \item middleboxes on the path that drop IP fragments;
    \item how often the procedures are repeated over the lifetime of
    a tunnel.
\end{itemize}
The published measurements reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:sota-impl} cover only
the initial establishment, and most of them change more than the key
exchange at a time. None of them emulates the constrained links of cellular
IoT.

The thesis therefore asks four questions.
\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1 (establishment).] Compared with classical X25519, what do pure
    ML-KEM (512, 768, 1024) and hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM cost when an IKE SA is
    established? The cost covers messages and bytes on the wire, IP and IKE
    fragmentation, set-up time, and the CPU time and memory of the IKE
    daemon. Throughput and latency of the protected traffic are measured as
    a control.
    \item[RQ2 (rekeying).] What does each rekeying procedure cost with these
    configurations: a Child SA rekey with and without key exchange, an IKE SA
    rekey, and reauthentication?
    \item[RQ3 (constraints).] How do constrained links and a resource-limited
    initiator change these costs, and under which conditions does
    establishment fail? Constrained links here means delay, loss, a low rate,
    a small MTU, and middleboxes that drop IP fragments.
    \item[RQ4 (guidance).] What configuration and migration guidance follows,
    in general and for the telecom, IoT and financial-sector use cases?
\end{description}
RQ1 and RQ2 form the generic core of the thesis. RQ3 extends it to the
constrained links and devices of IoT deployments. RQ4 is answered in
Chapter~\ref{sec:reco}, from the results of the first three.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:analysis-scope}

The core of the thesis is generic: the cost of ML-KEM in the key exchange of
IKEv2. Three use cases give it context and shape the test conditions. They are
described from public sources only.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Telecom.} Base stations protect their backhaul with IPsec
    towards a security gateway that terminates many IKE SAs. GSMA PQ.06
    reports a hybrid prototype of this setting with up to 520 IKE
    SAs~\cite{gsmaPq06}. The thesis therefore also measures many SAs towards
    one responder.
    \item \textbf{IoT.} Gateways connect over cellular links such as LTE-M and
    NB-IoT, with long round-trip times, low rates and loss, and they have
    limited CPU and memory (Section~\ref{sec:sota-iot}). The thesis therefore
    emulates such links and a resource-limited initiator.
    \item \textbf{Financial sector.} Branches connect to the VPN concentrator
    of a head office over site-to-site tunnels, under regulation that requires
    cryptography to follow the state of the art
    (Section~\ref{sec:sota-regulatory}). The thesis therefore carries traffic
    between networks behind the gateways and examines rekeying policy and
    firewalls that drop IP fragments.
\end{itemize}

The thesis covers KEM-based key exchange, pure and hybrid ML-KEM, for the
initial IKE SA and for every procedure of Table~\ref{tab:rtt}. It uses an
unmodified, released implementation in a test bed of virtual machines with
emulated network conditions. The following are out of scope:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-quantum authentication}
    (Section~\ref{sec:sota-pqauth}). Authentication stays classical and
    identical in every configuration, so that only the key exchange varies.
    \item \textbf{Post-quantum preshared keys} (Section~\ref{sec:sota-ppk}).
    They need no new algorithm and add almost no bytes, so there is little to
    measure. Chapter~\ref{sec:reco} discusses them as a migration option.
    % OPEN (supervisor meeting): confirm that PPKs stay out of the matrix.
    \item \textbf{Changes to the protocol or the implementation}, such as
    selective retransmission of fragments or composite keys over TCP
    (Section~\ref{sec:sota-impl}). The thesis measures what an operator can
    deploy today.
    \item \textbf{Energy consumption and real IoT hardware.} Energy cannot be
    measured meaningfully in virtual machines, and the resource-limited
    initiator is an emulation (Section~\ref{sec:analysis-vpn}).
    % OPEN (supervisor meeting): a Raspberry Pi as a real ARM gateway, as an
    % optional extension.
    \item \textbf{Production infrastructure.} All experiments run in an
    isolated lab.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Selection of the IPsec implementation and the test platform}
\label{sec:analysis-vpn}

\paragraph{Criteria for the implementation.}
From requirements F1--F9 and N1--N2, the IKEv2 implementation must:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item support all three ML-KEM parameter sets, both in
    \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT} and as an additional key exchange;
    \item implement RFC~7383, RFC~9242 and RFC~9370, including
    \texttt{IKE\_FOLLOWUP\_KE}, so that every procedure of
    Table~\ref{tab:rtt} works with a hybrid;
    \item be open source, released, actively maintained and usable without
    modification;
    \item offer a programmatic control interface for scripted runs and for
    timestamped events;
    \item be observable, with detailed logs and, in the lab, export of the
    session keys;
    \item run on Linux on arm64 and x86.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Choice.}
Section~\ref{sec:sota-impl} identified two open-source daemons that implement
the standardised hybrid key exchange, strongSwan and Libreswan
(Table~\ref{tab:impl}). A third open-source daemon, OpenBSD's iked, offers
post-quantum key exchange only as \texttt{sntrup761\allowbreak x25519}, a hybrid group
outside the IKEv2 standards, and implements neither ML-KEM nor
RFC~9370~\cite{openbsdIked}, so it fails criteria~1 and~2. strongSwan and
Libreswan both meet criteria~2 and~3. The first criterion
decides: Libreswan~5.4 implements ML-KEM-768 only~\cite{libreswan}, so it
cannot run the ML-KEM-512 and ML-KEM-1024 configurations. strongSwan has met
criteria~1 and~2 since version~6.0~\cite{strongswan}. It also offers the VICI
control interface for criterion~4 and a \texttt{save-keys} plugin that writes
the IKE and ESP session keys in a format that Wireshark can read, for
criterion~5. Its \texttt{load-tester} plugin can open many IKE SAs for
requirement~F7. The thesis therefore uses strongSwan. It is built from an
unmodified release, using strongSwan's own ML-KEM implementation, whereas
several studies in Table~\ref{tab:relwork} added liboqs or modified the
daemon~\cite{bae2023,twardokus2025,he2025,rahul2026}.
Chapter~\ref{sec:impl} gives the version and how it was verified.

\paragraph{Test platform.}
The test bed consists of virtual machines on a single laptop, as agreed with
the supervisor. Each gateway runs in its own virtual machine with its own
kernel and IPsec stack, as it would on a real device. Its CPU and memory can be
limited and measured separately. A third virtual machine routes all traffic
between the gateways. It applies the network conditions of requirement~F5 and
captures every packet with a single clock (N4), so neither the impairment nor
the capture loads a measured host. The whole set-up is created by scripts, so
it can be rebuilt from scratch (N3), and it needs no hardware beyond the
laptop (N6).

The choice has consequences that limit how the results can be interpreted.
The laptop is an Apple M3 Pro with performance and efficiency cores, and
macOS does not allow virtual CPUs to be pinned to either kind. Absolute timings
and CPU figures are therefore those of this host, not of a gateway appliance,
and the comparison between configurations matters more than the absolute
values. The virtual machines run arm64 Linux, the same instruction set as
many IoT gateways, but not their performance class. The resource-limited
initiator of requirement~F6 is emulated with control-group limits, which
throttle the daemon in bursts rather than slowing every instruction. Energy
cannot be measured. Chapter~\ref{sec:method} describes how these effects are
controlled, and Chapter~\ref{sec:results} discusses them as threats to
validity.

\subsection{Selection of algorithms and parameter sets}
\label{sec:analysis-algorithms}

Table~\ref{tab:configs} lists the configurations. They differ only in the key
exchange (N1).

\paragraph{Classical baseline.}
The baseline C1 uses X25519. It is the smallest and fastest classical method
available in IKEv2, with 32 bytes per party (Table~\ref{tab:ke-sizes}), so it
is the most demanding baseline against which to show the overhead of ML-KEM.
It also avoids the comparison with MODP-2048 made by the closest previous
study~\cite{twardokus2025}. A second baseline with the NIST curve P-256 (C2)
is kept as an option for environments that require NIST curves.

\paragraph{Pure ML-KEM.}
P512, P768 and P1024 put ML-KEM in \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT}. The ML-KEM draft
allows this without reservation only for ML-KEM-512. It advises against
ML-KEM-768 and ML-KEM-1024 there when the path MTU is unknown or small,
because \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT} cannot be fragmented at the IKE
layer~\cite{ikev2mlkem}. The thesis includes all three parameter sets on
purpose: what happens when this advice is not followed is part of what RQ1
and RQ3 measure.

\paragraph{Hybrid.}
H512, H768 and H1024 keep X25519 in \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT} and add ML-KEM as
the first additional key exchange in \texttt{IKE\_INTERMEDIATE}. The classical
component is the same in all three, so only the ML-KEM parameter set varies.
ML-KEM-512, 768 and 1024 target NIST security categories~1, 3 and~5
respectively~\cite{fips203}, while X25519 is intended for about 128~bits of
classical security~\cite{rfc8031}. This mismatch is acceptable, because the classical component of
a hybrid protects against a failure of ML-KEM rather than adding post-quantum
strength (Section~\ref{sec:sota-quantum}). Pairs matched in classical
strength, such as P-384 + ML-KEM-1024, are a possible second axis if time
allows. H768 is the main configuration, because NIST recommends ML-KEM-768 as
the default parameter set~\cite[Section~8]{fips203}.

\paragraph{Parameters kept constant.}
All configurations use the same IKE SA proposal apart from the key exchange:
AES-256-GCM with a 16-byte ICV and HMAC-SHA2-384 as the PRF. ESP uses
AES-256-GCM. A 256-bit symmetric key keeps the data plane secure against
Grover's algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:sota-quantum}). The peers authenticate
with ECDSA P-256 certificates issued by a lab certification authority. These
certificates and signatures are small, so \texttt{IKE\_AUTH} fits into
a single packet, and any fragmentation observed comes from the key exchange.
The ESP proposal carries a key exchange only in the scenario that measures
a Child SA rekey with key exchange. There it uses the key exchange of the
configuration under test. Rekeying is triggered explicitly by the measurement
scripts rather than by lifetimes, so that each procedure runs exactly when it
is measured.

\subsection{Expected effects}
\label{sec:analysis-hypotheses}

The theory of Chapter~\ref{sec:sota} predicts the following effects. They are
stated before any measurement, so that the results in
Chapter~\ref{sec:results} can confirm or refute them.
\begin{description}
    \item[H1 (RQ1, messages and bytes).] The IKE messages grow by the
    key-exchange data of Table~\ref{tab:configs}. Pure configurations keep
    two exchanges but enlarge \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT}. Hybrids leave
    \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT} almost unchanged and add one
    \texttt{IKE\_INTERMEDIATE} exchange. \texttt{IKE\_AUTH} is the same in
    every configuration.
    \item[H2 (RQ1, size limits).] Headers and the other payloads add a few
    hundred bytes to the KE payload. The \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT} messages of
    P1024 therefore exceed a 1500-byte MTU and must be fragmented at the IP
    layer. Those of P768 fit into 1500 bytes but not into 1280, and those of
    P512 fit into both. Hybrid messages larger than the IKE fragment size are
    split by RFC~7383 instead.
    \item[H3 (RQ1, time and CPU).] On an unimpaired path, set-up time is
    dominated by round trips and fixed processing rather than by ML-KEM
    computation. Hybrids, with one more round trip, therefore take longer than
    pure configurations with the same parameter set. The CPU time per
    handshake grows only moderately. Throughput and latency of ESP do not
    change, because the data plane does not depend on the key exchange.
    \item[H4 (RQ2).] A Child SA rekey without key exchange costs the same in
    every configuration and serves as a control. Procedures with a key
    exchange behave like the initial establishment. A hybrid adds one
    \texttt{IKE\_FOLLOWUP\_KE} round trip (Table~\ref{tab:rtt}). A pure
    configuration adds no round trip, and because \texttt{CREATE\_CHILD\_SA}
    is encrypted, its larger messages can be fragmented at the IKE layer.
    Reauthentication costs about as much as the initial establishment.
    \item[H5 (RQ3).] Delay amplifies the extra round trip of hybrids. Loss
    amplifies the number of packets per message, because IKEv2 retransmits
    whole messages (Section~\ref{sec:sota-impl}). On a path that drops IP
    fragments, every configuration whose \texttt{IKE\_SA\_INIT} needs IP
    fragmentation fails to establish~\cite{rfc7383}. Hybrids, which carry
    ML-KEM in messages that can be fragmented at the IKE layer, still succeed.
    A CPU-limited initiator stretches the computational part of the set-up
    time.
\end{description}
